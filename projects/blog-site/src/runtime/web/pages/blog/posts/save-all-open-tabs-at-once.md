Somewhere past the twentieth tab, the titles vanish. The strip fills with favicons, each one a page opened with every intention of getting read.

None of them get read that day. A handful survive into the weekend and get opened. The rest stay put, held by a reason that feels solid in the moment: closing the tab means losing the page, and losing the page means the reading never happens.

So the window stays open. It carries into tomorrow, then into next week. The browser reloads after an update and asks whether to restore 40 tabs, and the answer is always yes, because the alternative feels like admitting the reading is already gone.

## Why the window stays open

An open tab is the most common read-later tool in the world, and one of the worst. It works by keeping the page in front of you, which is the same thing that makes it heavy. Every tab holds a little memory and a little attention. 40 of them turn the browser slow and the strip unreadable, and still none of it counts as read.

Bookmarks were supposed to fix this. They don't, and most people with a tab problem also have a bookmarks folder they never open. A bookmark saves the address and nothing else, so opening it later means loading the live page again, paywall and pop-ups and all, and hoping the piece is still where you left it. The tab at least holds the page you already saw. That is why the tab wins and the window keeps growing.

There's a quieter cost under all of it. The tab lives in the one place you can't afford to lose. A crash, a forced restart, a session that wipes on its own, and the whole list is gone with no copy kept anywhere. A reading pile that exists only while the browser stays awake is a pile you will lose, and you tend to find out at the worst time.

> **A tab keeps the page in front of you, which is the same thing that makes it heavy.**

## One shortcut takes the whole window

The Readplace extension now saves the whole window at once. Press Ctrl+Shift+D, or Cmd+Shift+D on a Mac, and it walks every tab in the current window and sends each page to your reading queue.

It captures each page as it stands, the same clean reader copy you get when you save one link by hand. A tab behind a login you are already through, or an article that would meet a paywall the second time around, saves the version you can actually read. The title, the text, and a short summary land on the card over the next moments, the way a single save fills in on its own.

A big window doesn't choke on the way out. The extension sends the pages in groups rather than all in one request, so a window of 60 tabs uploads in steady batches instead of one heavy upload that stalls. The save is safe to repeat, so running it twice on the same window never adds a page twice.

A small panel counts as it runs: Saved 31, Skipped 4. When it finishes, a View Queue button opens the list, now holding what the window held. You close the tabs behind you.

## It keeps the reading, not the clutter

Not every tab is an article. Your email sits open, a settings page, a `chrome://` page, the Readplace queue itself. None of those belong in a reading list, so the extension drops them before it sends anything. Two tabs open on the same address collapse to a single save, so a page you opened twice saves, counts, and captures once.

Very large pages get handled rather than dropped. A tab whose captured content runs past 20 MB, often a long thread or a page thick with inline media, saves as a plain link instead of a full copy, and the panel names it so you can see which ones went in light. The rest save whole. Nothing fails without a word.

## A tab was the wrong place to keep it

The reason to keep a tab open was never the tab. It was the reading behind it, the piece you meant to get to. The tab was only the holder within reach, so it did a job it was bad at, in the one spot where losing it costs the most.

> **Move the reading into a queue and the tab has nothing left to hold.**

You can close the window, restart the machine, or let the browser wipe the session, and the list stays on Readplace's side. Each page is a clean copy that opens the same next month as it does today. Closing a tab goes back to meaning what it should, the end of a page you are done with, rather than a small loss you keep choosing to avoid.
