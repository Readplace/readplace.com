Somewhere past the fifteenth tab, the strip at the top of the browser stops showing titles. Each one shrinks to a favicon, then to a sliver, until the row is a wall of squares too small to read. The tabs stay open anyway.

They stay open because each one is something you meant to read and have not yet.

An article you opened at lunch and got two paragraphs into. The piece it linked to that looked worth keeping. Closing a tab feels like deciding the thing inside it does not matter, so the tab stays, and the window fills up instead.

What you have built, without meaning to, is a reading list. It is just a bad one.

The browser was not made to hold one, so it cannot search the pile and it forgets the lot the moment it restarts. Restart the laptop and a careful stack of intentions is gone at once.

> **The open tabs are a reading list the browser was not built to hold.**

## What one move does to the window

Right-click any page and pick Save All Tabs to Readplace. The extension reads every tab in the current window, sends the real articles to your queue, and answers with a count: Saved 18 · Skipped 4. After that the window is safe to close.

The reading is in Readplace, off the source pages, and the row of squares is gone. Nothing got triaged by hand.

The manual version of this is real work. You save one tab, switch back, save the next, and keep at it until you lose count or give up around tab 12. A window that took a week to fill comes apart by hand slowly, if at all. One right-click clears it in a few seconds.

There is a keyboard shortcut too, Ctrl+Shift+D, or Command+Shift+D on a Mac. Both browsers already reserve that combination for their own bookmark-all-tabs command, so it can arrive unassigned. The right-click item works with no setup, and you can bind your own key in the browser's shortcut settings if you would rather reach for one.

## What it skips, and why that is the point

It does not save every tab open in the window. Your email tab, the settings page you left open, Readplace's own pages, the chrome:// screens no extension is allowed to read: those come back as Skipped, not as failures. Two tabs pointed at the same article save once.

> **The count is the articles you meant to read, not the noise open around them.**

A page too heavy to capture in full, some long thread with every reply loaded inline, saves as a plain link instead, and the popup names which ones and how big they were. So a giant page still lands in your queue. It just lands as a link the server reads for itself, the same way a single save works.

Each saved tab shows up right away as a card with the site name, then fills in its title, a short summary, and the clean reader copy over the next moments. That is the pipeline one save runs through, so a window of 30 tabs becomes 30 cards at once and finishes on its own. You read them later off Readplace's copy, not the live pages, so a source that changes or goes dark does not empty what you kept.

## Closing a tab you have already saved

The tabs were not the reading list. They were the worry that closing one meant losing it.

A window with 40 tabs is 40 small refusals to let go, propped up by a browser that will drop the lot the next time it restarts. Saving the window hands that worry somewhere better to sit. The articles are kept on Readplace's servers, the way a saved link [stays readable after the original page is gone](/blog/saved-articles-outlast-the-original-page), and the window goes back to empty.

The list is still there when you want it, in order and searchable for once, waiting for the day you read it rather than the day the laptop restarts and wipes it.

Next time the strip is more squares than words, the [Readplace extension for Chrome and Firefox](https://readplace.com/install) folds the whole window into your reading list in one right-click.
